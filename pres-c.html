Something already changed.

For decades, AI was a brain in a jar.
It could think. It could speak. It couldn't do.

Then — not with a press conference,
but with a software update — it got hands.

Brief. Set up the "how" with minimal words. "And once it had hands, the question stopped being 'is it smart?' and started being 'what do I let it do?'"

The architecture of doing

This is how you work on a hard problem. Now machines work this way too.

Walk the loop once: goal, context, plan, act, observe, evaluate. "This is the architecture of doing. Not one prompt. A loop." Show the mechanism early so everything after is grounded.

Mini-case: hiring a freelancer

Before (~4h over 2 days)

Write job description

Post on 2-3 platforms

Review 15+ portfolios

Shortlist 3-4 candidates

Send outreach messages

With agent (~45 min)

Drafts job post from project brief

Searches platforms, ranks portfolios

Drafts shortlist with reasoning

Prepares personalized outreach

Human reviews, adjusts, approves

The agent did the work. You made every decision.

This example is about delegation specifically. It models the talk's central question. "Notice: the agent can't send anything. You approve every outbound action." "The question isn't 'can it do this?' It's 'am I comfortable letting it?'"

Everyone has a line. The question is: what determines where it falls?

Interactive moment. Let the audience place themselves. "Look at that spectrum. Where did your gut put you?" "The line isn't about capability. It's about stakes, reversibility, and who's accountable." "That's the trust question."

The trust equation

Scope

what it's allowed to do

Visibility

can I see what it did and why

Reversibility

can I undo a mistake quickly

This is the same framework you use for human delegation.
Manager to employee. Parent to teenager.
The variables are identical. The delegate is new.

This is the key insight of the talk. "Delegation isn't new. We've been doing it forever. Scope, visibility, reversibility." "The only thing that changed is WHO you're delegating to."

Five things people confuse

Intelligence — can it reason?

Capability — what can it do?

Agency — can it act in the world?

Autonomy — does it act without asking?

Reliability — does it get it right?

A brain in a jar: high intelligence, zero agency.
A Roomba: high agency, mediocre intelligence.
The interesting zone is where they overlap.

Run through each quickly. "When people argue about AGI, they're arguing about five different things using one word." The brain-in-jar vs Roomba contrast is the memorable line. Land it.

General = transferable

The same system that found your freelancer could also:

research a legal question
prepare a financial summary
draft a project plan
debug your code

Not because it learned each task —
because it reasons about goals and tools.

Tie directly back to the hiring example. "General doesn't mean omniscient. It means transferable." "One system, moving across domains by reasoning about what to do and how."

The dangers of hands

confident wrong actions — it doesn't hesitate

permission creep — small grants compound into large access

quiet failures — errors nobody notices until they matter

accountability blur — "who did this?" has no clear answer

"These aren't future risks. They're happening right now, in every team using agents." Calm tone. Not fear. Clarity. "Remember the trust equation? This is what happens when you skip it."

What humans are left holding

Judgment — is this good enough?

Taste — is this what we actually want?

Values — should we be doing this at all?

Responsibility — it acted, but I'm accountable

The bottleneck was effort. Now the bottleneck is meaning.

"When the doing is delegated, these are what remain. These are the human parts." "Notice: none of these can be automated. They require a person who cares."

The questions behind the question

What work is worth doing ourselves — even when we don't have to?

What does craft mean when the craft is delegated?

Where does pride of authorship live now?

SLOW DOWN. This is the quiet, slow slide. Let it breathe. "These aren't technical questions. They're human questions. And they don't have answers yet." Pause after each one. Let the room sit with it.
