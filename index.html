A brain in a jar

"For sixty years, artificial intelligence was a brain in a jar. It could think. It could speak. It could pass tests, write poetry, explain quantum physics. But it couldn't DO anything. It had no hands. Then — quietly, not with a press conference but with a software update — it got them. And that changes everything."

Now

Goal → Plan → Act → Learn

"Here's the shift in one picture. Before: you ask a question, you get an answer. That's a search engine with better grammar. Now: you describe what you want done. The AI makes a plan. It picks up tools. It acts. It checks whether it worked. It tries again if it didn't. That's not a better chatbot. That's a different kind of thing entirely."

What people argue about

Intelligence — Can it reason?

Capability — What can it do?

Agency — Can it act?

Autonomy — Does it act alone?

Reliability — Can we trust it?

"Everyone argues about AGI — Artificial General Intelligence. Will it happen? When? Is it dangerous? But most of those arguments are confused, because people are mixing up five different things. Intelligence: can it reason well? That's one question. Capability: what tasks can it perform? Different question. Agency: can it act in the world — touch files, send emails, use tools? Different again. Autonomy: does it act without asking permission? Reliability: when it acts, can we trust the result? You can have high intelligence with zero agency. That's a philosophy professor. You can have high agency with mediocre intelligence. That's a Roomba. The interesting — and the slightly unsettling — zone is where they start to overlap."

Agency changes your Tuesday

"'General intelligence' gets the headlines. But agency is what changes your Tuesday. An AI that can reason beautifully but can't touch anything? That's interesting at a dinner party. An AI that can read your email, check your calendar, draft a reply, research a question, and send a follow-up — all while you're in the shower? That's an operator. The shift from interface to actor is the real story. Not 'is it smart enough?' but 'can it do things in the world?' That's already happening. Right now."

The architecture of doing

"This is the agent loop. It's the architecture of doing — not just for AI, but for everything that acts. Start with a goal. Gather context. Make a plan. Act — actually do something in the world. Observe what happened. Evaluate: did it work? Do I need to try again? This is how humans work. You don't write an email in one draft with no re-reading. You draft, review, revise, send. Maybe check if they replied. Now machines work this way too. Not one prompt, one response. A loop of intention, action, and feedback. That's agency."

General ≠ omniscient

General means transferable

"'General' doesn't mean it knows everything. It means it can transfer. The same system that writes code can also research your competitors, draft outreach emails, analyze a spreadsheet, plan a trip, and debug a server. Not because it was trained on each task separately — but because it can reason about tools and goals in the abstract. That's what 'general' actually means. And we're much closer than most people realize. We may spend years arguing about whether we've achieved AGI while the real transition is already reshaping daily life."

Small shifts in friction

"The future doesn't arrive as a movie scene. No robots marching down the street. It arrives as small changes in friction, timing, and expectation. 'I'll look into it' — already looked into. Your agent checked while you were talking. 'Let me draft something' — the draft is waiting when you sit down. 'I need to hire someone for that' — maybe you don't. The meaning of effort gets blurrier. Did you write that email, or did you approve it? Did you research that topic, or did you read the summary your system made? These are small changes. But they compound. And they're happening now."

The uncanny middle

"Right now, we're in the uncanny middle. AI sounds like a person, but it isn't one. It acts like an employee, but it has no stake in the outcome. It makes mistakes that feel human — but they're alien errors. Confident, consistent, and wrong in ways no human would be. We haven't built the social norms yet. We're improvising. When your AI sends an email on your behalf, who wrote it? What does 'I' mean in that email? This is the weird part. We're living through it right now, and we're making up the rules as we go."

What it reveals about us

What is judgment vs. routine?

What work is worth doing ourselves?

What does "I did this" mean?

"Here's what I find most interesting. As we build agents, we're forced to answer questions we've been avoiding for decades. What is judgment versus routine? If an AI can do 80% of a job, what's the 20% that actually requires a human? What work is worth doing ourselves — not because we have to, but because the doing is part of the meaning? What does 'I did this' mean when your agent did the work and you approved it? AI doesn't just automate tasks. It pressurizes our definitions of skill, craft, and dignity. It holds up a mirror and asks: what is it that you actually do?"

This has happened before

Electricity reorganized factories

Internet reorganized media

Agentic AI reorganizes work itself

"This kind of reorganization has happened before. Electricity didn't just make factories brighter. It let you put a motor on every machine instead of running everything off one central steam shaft. That changed the entire layout of factories, then cities, then society. The internet didn't just make newspapers digital. It changed who could publish, what 'news' meant, how information moved. Agentic AI reorganizes work itself. Not just 'faster typing' — but what it means to have a team, to delegate, to manage. The competitive unit shifts from 'who can do the work' to 'who can direct the system.'"

What to watch for

When you stop doing and start checking

When small teams outperform large ones

When "I'll handle it" means something different

When your first non-human colleague arrives

"So what should you pay attention to? Not the benchmarks. Not the model names. Watch for when you stop doing a task and start checking that it was done correctly. That's the shift from operator to supervisor. Watch for small teams — three people — outperforming companies of thirty. Because those three have agents. Watch for when 'I'll handle it' starts meaning 'I'll tell my system to handle it.' And watch for the first business you interact with that has more agents than employees. That's coming sooner than you think."
