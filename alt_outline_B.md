# Alt Outline B — "Show First, Explain After"

**Arc:** Live example → Mechanism → Implications → Human meaning
**Thesis:** Don't explain what agents are — show what they do, then unpack.
**Tone:** Concrete and surprising, then reflective.

---

## Slide 1 — Title

**Clawd: When Intelligence Grew Hands**
Otto von Wachter

---

## Slide 2 — "Let me show you something."

No theory. One concrete scenario:

"Last week I needed to prepare a presentation about a topic I hadn't researched. I described what I wanted. Thirty minutes later, the outline existed, the research was done, the slides were drafted, and I was editing — not creating."

Speaker note: Start with a specific, true moment. Audience should think: "Wait, how?"

---

## Slide 3 — What just happened? (The agent loop)

Goal → Context → Plan → Act → Observe → Evaluate → Repeat

"That wasn't a chatbot. It was a loop — the same loop you use when you work on a project. Set a goal, gather context, make a plan, try something, check the result, adjust."

Speaker note: Diagram slide. Explain the mechanism immediately after the demo.

---

## Slide 4 — What I mean by "hands"

Hands = access to tools:
- write / edit files and documents
- browse the web and research
- run code, scripts, workflows
- call APIs and external services
- loop: try, observe, adjust, repeat

Speaker note: Keep it concrete. People relax when it's tangible.

---

## Slide 5 — Mini-case: Bug triage and release notes

**The task:** Scan 47 recent bug reports, categorize severity, draft release notes for the next version.

**Before (~3 hours):**
- Read each report individually
- Cross-reference with codebase
- Write severity assessments
- Compile into formatted release notes

**With agent (~40 min):**
- Agent reads all 47 reports, clusters by component
- Cross-references code changes automatically
- Drafts severity tiers with reasoning
- Human reviews classifications, adjusts 4-5 edge cases, approves

**Key insight:** The human's job became quality control and editorial judgment — not data processing.

Speaker note: Use a technical example — shows this isn't just summarizing text. The agent actually reasoned about code.

---

## Slide 6 — General ≠ omniscient

General means *transferable*.

The same system that triaged bugs also:
- Researched competitors
- Drafted outreach emails
- Analyzed a spreadsheet
- Planned a trip

Not because it was trained on each task — because it can reason about tools and goals.

Speaker note: Connect directly back to the examples. "General" becomes concrete, not abstract.

---

## Slide 7 — The shift in one sentence

Before: Question → Answer (a tool)
Now: Goal → Plan → Act → Learn (a worker)

"That's not a better chatbot. That's a different kind of thing."

---

## Slide 8 — Why AGI debates miss the point

Everyone argues about "AGI" but they're mixing up:
- Intelligence (can it reason?)
- Capability (what can it do?)
- Agency (can it act?)
- Autonomy (does it act unasked?)
- Reliability (can we trust the results?)

"You can have high intelligence with zero agency — a brain in a jar.
You can have high agency with mediocre intelligence — a Roomba.
The interesting zone is where they overlap."

Speaker note: Brain-in-jar / Roomba contrast is memorable. Use it.

---

## Slide 9 — The dangers (without fear-mongering)

The real risks aren't sci-fi:
- Confident wrong actions (it doesn't doubt itself)
- Permission creep (small grants compound)
- Quiet failures (no one notices until it matters)
- Accountability blur ("Who did this?" "...the system?")

"This is like giving a new employee admin access on day one."

---

## Slide 10 — The trust framework

The question shifts:
"Is it smart?" → "Can I trust it?"

Trust = three things:
- **Scope** — what it's allowed to do
- **Visibility** — what it did and why
- **Reversibility** — can we undo mistakes

Speaker note: This is the practical takeaway for anyone managing or using agents.

---

## Slide 11 — What changes in us

Humans move toward: judgment, taste, values, responsibility
Less time on: rote execution, coordination overhead

"The bottleneck becomes meaning and choice — not effort."

---

## Slide 12 — The reorganization

This has happened before:
- Electricity reorganized factories (central shaft → distributed motors)
- The internet reorganized media (broadcast → network)
- Agentic AI reorganizes work (human execution → human direction)

"The competitive unit shifts from 'who can do the work' to 'who can direct the system.'"

---

## Slide 13 — What to watch for

Not benchmarks. Not model names. Watch for:
- When you stop doing a task and start *checking* it was done
- When small teams outperform large ones (again)
- When "I'll handle it" means something different
- When the first business you use has more agents than employees

---

## Slide 14 — Closing

"The future is not AI replacing humans.
It's humans learning to direct intelligence that acts.
The hands are here. The question is what we build with them."

---

**Why this ordering might work better:**
- Opens with a concrete, vivid moment — audience is hooked by specificity, not abstraction
- Explains mechanism (agent loop) immediately when curiosity is highest
- Two concrete examples before any theory (presentation + bug triage)
- AGI discussion arrives late, as context — not as the centerpiece
- The "reorganization" slide adds historical weight near the end
- 14 slides — slightly longer but each earns its place with new information
